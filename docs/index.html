Kaggriculture Agent

A hand-built heuristic farming agent for a two-player simulation competition, and an honest look at why the winners were neural networks.

Built for Kaggle's Kaggriculture featured simulation competition (2026), closed 2026-09-30. Source: github.com/Alherma7/AI-agent-Kaggriculture.

The problem

Two players each run a 10×10 farm for 30 in-game days of 24 turns (720 turns). Every turn each unit (one farmer plus any hands hired that day) gets one action, and the player sends up to 10 market orders. Money comes from crops (wheat, carrot, tomato, strawberry, melon) and animals (goose, cow, sheep), sold into a market shared with the opponent whose prices fall as supply grows. Most money at the end wins.

Three facts shaped the whole project, though we only understood the last one late:

  • Most invalid actions fail silently. A PLANT on a locked tile, a HARVEST before first_yield_day, an over-requested seed: all no-ops with no error. Most of our bugs were in this class.
  • The market is processed order by order, in lockstep with the opponent. The position of an order in our list changes the price it gets.
  • The ladder only scores win, draw or loss. The final ranking is a rating over about two weeks of post-deadline games. The money margin never counts. We optimized average money against our own previous agent.

The final agent

1. Crop calendar. Melon for days 0–9 (high price while the market is fresh), then wheat as the replanted staple. Carrot, a one-off batch from day 4. Strawberry on a fixed tile and on one NE zone.
↓
2. Units on fixed routes. The farmer walks a closed 12-tile loop (the ~12 tiles/day a unit can tend). Up to 9 hands are hired in a batch at the start of each day, each on a private, disjoint zone, so they never merge onto the same tile.
↓
3. Land. NE quadrant bought from day 6 once it can be afforded (lands around day 7), with dedicated extra hands rather than relocated ones.
↓
4. Animals as chain hands. Three hands each walk a closed circuit of pastures (14 tiles total, hugging the spawn row like the strong replays). Each picks up one wheat per pasture in a single shed trip, then builds, places, feeds, cares for and harvests along the circuit. Animal purchases follow placement capacity.
↓
5. Market. Sells go first in the order list, before any buys. The whole shed is sold at once. A wheat floor keeps the herd fed. Hires are batched into one turn.

Everything lives in one large rule-based function in notebooks/15_leaderboard_agent.ipynb, flattened into agent/main.py by scripts/build_agent.py, a tested AST extractor.

The key finding: mechanics beat strategy

The three largest effects in the project weren't strategic ideas. They were mechanics the agent was getting wrong:

  1. How much wheat a hand picks up per trip (+47k). We first blamed tile geometry ("only 4 tiles near the shed"). Tracing 3 top replays hour by hour showed the real bottleneck was the pickup quantity.
  2. Where SELL sits in the order list (+7k). The 4th-place team discovered the same mechanic after the deadline, and estimated it at $0.4–0.6k per game against the top 3. We had it on Sep 1.
  3. How many HIREs go out per turn (+7k). An if/elif chain left over from an earlier refactor.

All of them were found by instrumenting an episode turn by turn, often by patching the engine to print state before and after an action. Reading the code never surfaced them. Most were silent no-ops: the engine accepts the action and does nothing, so you only see them in the money curve.

Lessons

Optimize the metric the ladder actually scores

The ladder rewards wins, not money, and it rates you against a field that keeps moving. We gated every change on "does it beat our own previous agent", and later on mean money over 8 seeds. That is a self-referential yardstick: it proved we kept improving, never how far we were from the field. A small league of public agents from day one, scored by wins on paired seeds, would have shown the 3–5× gap weeks earlier.

Measure with paired seeds from the start

Early runs reused one env, so all n episodes were bit-identical, and the real variance (~5k per episode, from the shared market) only showed between separate calls. We switched to paired 8-seed A/B in September. Every number before that is a head-to-head result, not an effect size.

Build a "did this action do anything?" checker early

Most bugs were actions the engine silently ignored. scripts/replay_guard.py (silent failures and milestone drift) arrived on the last day. 3rd place shipped exactly this idea as inference-time post-processing: apply each move to a copy of the state with the official rule functions, and fix only the moves that provably change nothing.

Copy the economics, not the calendar

Lifting a top replay's day-by-day timeline (land on day 6, strawberry on day 4) failed silently whenever our agent couldn't afford it that day. Copying the replay's milestones to the day cost −22k when the money behind them wasn't there.

Recognize the paradigm early

In a simulation competition with public replays of strong players, imitation learning is the baseline, not an advanced option. By late August our analysis already showed top agents converging on near-identical play: the "meta" signal BC exploits. We kept turning those patterns into hand-written rules, each costing a day of zone geometry and bug hunting, while the same data could have trained a policy. The Level 4 "learning" item, "only if heuristics plateau", was placed too late in the plan.

Hard-coded geometry doesn't scale

Fixed zones per hand meant every new crop, animal or quadrant reopened routing: KeyErrors when land unlocked mid-day, checkerboard parity limits, hands walking locked tiles. Winners used a generic "pick a target tile, walk the shortest path" rule (4th and 7th place). A task-assignment layer would have made each increment cheap.

The last four weeks are the competition

We stopped on Sep 3, partly for lack of time and partly because the heuristic ceiling was showing. The field changed most in exactly that window: new versions almost daily, the 4-quadrant meta in the last three days. A heuristic tuned to the August meta decays. Next time: budget time so the final stretch is spent on iteration, not cut short.

What carried over well

The working discipline: one increment at a time, each gated before the next, every result logged including the negative ones (PROGRESS.md), engine source as ground truth over documentation, and a tested build step for the submission. That process found every mechanic in the key finding. What it lacked was the right yardstick and the right model class.

What this isn't: a claim that compute explains the gap. Farmcore and DECEM placed 4th and 7th with a single GPU and about an hour of training. What the top finishers shared was procedural:

  • learn from the strongest public games instead of hand-coding them;
  • evaluate by win/loss against opponents that react;
  • keep only a handful of rules, the ones that stop the engine from destroying goods.

Future: an RL version

Reinforcement learning sat on this project's roadmap as Level 4, "only if heuristics plateau", and was never reached. The heuristics did plateau, and RL fundamentals are the skill I want to build next. Kaggriculture is a good testbed for that: a deterministic engine, public replays of strong players, a fast community simulator, and detailed write-ups of what failed.

How the top teams used RL

Nobody won with pure RL. Everyone who tried PPO from scratch reported that it collapsed: 720 turns with a reward only at the end is too long a path for the signal to reach the early decisions. What worked was RL as a second stage on top of imitation:

PlaceHow RL was usedOutcome
1stBC → self-play PPO, alternated several times; a heuristic planner generated extra demonstrations~8.3M self-play games in the final model's lineage
3rdBC → PPO against itself and a league of past versions and rival clones"Most of the strength came from PPO"; up to 23 A100s
6thFrozen BC policy plays days 1–11; a PPO-trained policy takes over from day 12A shorter horizon made PPO tractable
7thEvolution strategies and PPO on the BC policy's conditioning embedding+$1k/game, submitted, but flipped too few games; final was pure BC
4thPPO with change in net worth as the reward+$1.3k/game, but ~10× less gain per GPU-hour than better data; dropped

The details that made RL work, repeated across write-ups:

  • Reward = final win/loss only. Shaped rewards (e.g. for buying land) broke the critic.
  • Clip the PPO ratio per decision, not per turn. A turn holds ~31 decisions (units + market slots); their joint probability ratio leaves the clip range at once and learning stalls.
  • A critic separate from the policy, antisymmetric between the two players (3rd place). Sharing the trunk let the value loss damage the policy.
  • A league and a promotion gate: train against past generations and rival clones, promote a new generation only if it beats the champion over many paired games.
  • Throughput first: transition-exact C++/Rust engines, batched games, and large rollouts, because one action's true effect ($30–400) is buried under ±$1–2k of noise.

Plan

Each stage ends with a measurable gate, the same discipline used throughout this project.

1. Fundamentals. MDPs, value iteration, tabular Q-learning (Taxi, FrozenLake); then REINFORCE and PPO on CartPole and LunarLander, first with Stable-Baselines3, then a single-file PPO written from scratch (CleanRL as reference).
Gate: my own PPO matches the library's learning curve on LunarLander.
↓
2. A reduced Kaggriculture. A Gym wrapper over a cut-down game: one player, no opponent, 1–3 days, farmer only, wheat only, a small discrete action space. Learn the delayed-reward problem at a horizon where it is still solvable.
Gate: PPO beats the hand-written wheat loop from notebook 03.
↓
3. Fast engine and evaluation. Adopt the community C++ port, check it transition by transition against the official engine, and build a paired-seed evaluator that counts win/loss flips against a league (this project's heuristic agents plus public ones).
Gate: identical final money on 100+ seeds; thousands of games per minute.
↓
4. Behavior cloning. Train a small Transformer on public replays of top teams, labelled with what the engine actually executed rather than what was ordered (4th place's key trick). Add the two rules every top team kept: sell the overflow before day end, sell everything on the last step.
Gate: beats this project's final heuristic agent on paired seeds.
↓
5. PPO on top of BC. Start with the 6th-place split: keep the BC opening and train PPO only from day 12 on, with a win/loss reward, per-decision clipping, a separate critic, and a KL penalty towards the BC policy. Then widen the window and add self-play against a league.
Gate: beats the BC policy it started from, over 1,000+ paired games, z > 2.
↓
6. Score it. Submit as a late submission and compare against the final leaderboard, and write up what transferred and what didn't.

Stages 1–2 need only a laptop CPU. Stages 4–5 fit on a single consumer GPU at small scale: 4th and 7th place trained their final models on one card.
